Continuous Futures & Roll Handling

Part 1 on TY (10y UST) and RX (Euro-Bund) · Part 2 on STIRs · daily data 2020-01 to 2026-09, 22 roots

1. The roll definition

I define the roll on open interest, because open interest is where positions are, which is the quantity the downstream signal is about. Within a cycle — the span over which generic 1 maps to one contract Ck and generic 2 to Ck+1 — let st = OIfront/(OIfront+OInext). Three dates, each the first of two consecutive sessions satisfying the condition: roll start s<0.85, roll date s<0.50, roll end s<0.15. The series changes contract at the settlement of the roll date. A crossing is only accepted if s was above 0.85 earlier in the same cycle, which makes the rule immune to a left-censored first cycle and to stale data. Thresholds act on a bounded ratio, so the same numbers run on a 3.9m-lot Treasury contract and a 150k-lot BTP contract unchanged — that is the whole point of the design, and section 6 is the test of it.

The alternatives, implemented and compared rather than dismissed. Volume crossover: a flow, not a stock, and calendar-spread trades print on both legs, so both are inflated during the roll week. It crosses a median 2–3 sessions after the OI rule and misfires outright on 1/26 TY and 2/26 RX cycles, where carried-forward volume at the provider's switch produces a spurious crossing. Calendar / first-notice: exact when the convention is right, and it agrees with my rule to within 1 session on all eleven bond roots — which is the evidence that the OI rule is picking up the real event. I did not choose it because it needs per-root delivery conventions, notice rules and an exchange holiday calendar, none of which is in the file, and it breaks silently when a contract changes spec or a new root is onboarded. It runs as a monitor (section 8), not as the definition.

2. The gap to the provider's generic (Part 1.2)

The provider's generic switches on the first session after the expiring contract's last trading day, not when the risk moves. The gap to my roll date is systematic, not noisy:

The split is entirely explained by the delivery mechanism. Contracts with a notice period force longs out at first notice (last business day of the month before delivery for CBOT), but keep trading for another three weeks as an illiquid stub; on TY the stub's open interest falls from 3.9m lots to 77 lots while generic 1 still points at it. Eurex contracts have no notice period, so liquidity stays until the last trading day and the provider's switch is nearly right. Consequence: for US Treasuries and Gilts, roughly 30–40% of all observations of "generic 1" describe a contract that no longer holds the risk. Any signal built on the raw front generic is wrong a third of the time, and the error is not random — it is concentrated in exactly the window a positioning signal cares about.

3. Continuous price, and what each method distorts

I build three series on my roll date, and judge them against a benchmark that cannot be wrong: the splice return of a position actually held and rolled, where both legs of every return come from the same contract. Roll gaps are always measured between the two contracts' settlements on the same day.

  • Difference (Panama) — primary. Reproduces price changes exactly (verified to 1e-9 in the test suite), so DV01 and points P&L are right. Distorts levels (history is shifted by the sum of all future gaps; on TY that sum is only −1.03 points over 27 rolls, 0.8% of price) and distorts percentage returns by up to 0.4–2 bp RMS.
  • Ratio — reproduces log returns exactly (1e-12), distorts differences, and is undefined if price touches zero. That is not hypothetical: CL settled at −37.63 on 2020-04-20.
  • Unadjusted provider generic 1 — wrong in both. It injects the calendar spread as a one-day return at each switch: 44.7 bp RMS / 120 bp max on TY (1.18 / 3.17 daily sigmas) and 115.8 bp / 278 bp on RX (2.66 / 6.39 sigmas).

For bonds I take difference adjustment: the roll gap is carry plus the cheapest-to-deliver option, it is a level effect of a few 32nds, and everything downstream is additive in price points. Here the choice barely matters (gap ~30 bp on TY); in crude it decides the series (section 7).

4. Continuous open interest

Generic-1 OI is not a positioning series — it collapses to zero every quarter by construction. I use whole-curve aggregate OI, with OI(1)+OI(2) as the fallback where no aggregate is published. For quarterly bond futures these are the same series: (OI1+OI2)/AGG has median exactly 1.00000 on every bond root, i.e. essentially all open interest sits in the front two contracts. The aggregate is preferred because it is immune to a data trap documented below. Positioning is then the normalised level — a 252-day z-score of log OI — and the front share st is reported separately as a roll-phase diagnostic, never as conviction. Migration is not a change in conviction and must not enter the same number.

5. Roll robustness: the metric and the result (Part 1.5)

Roll Artefact Score (RAS). Standardise daily changes by a 60-day trailing volatility estimated from non-roll days only, then RAS = median(|z| on roll days) / median(|z| elsewhere) − 1, with a 10,000-draw permutation p-value. RAS = 0 means a roll day is indistinguishable from an ordinary day. Medians, so one March-2020 session cannot carry the result. The same statistic runs on returns and on Δlog OI, and each construction is scored on the days it rolls — scoring the provider's series on my dates would flatter it to exactly zero. Alongside it: injected jump (RMS deviation from the splice benchmark on roll days) and calendar R² (share of the positioning signal's variance explained by roll-phase dummies — how much of the "signal" is just the calendar).

Removing the mean roll-phase shape takes calendar R² to 0.0006 while keeping 0.93 correlation with the raw signal — I report both and would let the downstream model choose. The damning number is the last column: a conviction signal built on generic-1 OI correlates 0.14 with the correct one on TY and 0.08 on RX. It is not a noisy version of the right signal; it is mostly a different, calendar-driven series.

6. Second bond future, nothing redesigned (Part 1.6)

RX run with identical thresholds, then all eleven bond roots swept. The definition holds: median roll length 3 sessions on every root; the calendar cross-check agrees to ≤1 session everywhere; difference adjustment is insignificant at the roll on 10 of 11 roots. What changes is the gap to the provider's generic (4–5 sessions on Eurex vs 19–26 on CBOT/ICE) — and that is a property of the contract, not a failure of the rule; the rule is what measures it. The one exception worth flagging: on IK (BTP) the difference-adjusted series scores RAS +0.45 with p = 0.043, marginally significant. BTP has the largest and most volatile roll gap of the set (82 bp mean, driven by CTD switches and sovereign-spread moves), so a single same-day gap is a noisier estimate of the true carry there. I would not use a single-day gap for BTP in production (section 8).

7. Part 2: STIRs — does "front vs next" even make sense?

No, and the data says so directly. On SOFR the front contract holds a median 10.7% of curve open interest (Euribor 13.6%, SONIA 11.2%); against 99.0% for TY and 99.7% for RX. The modal largest contract is the 2nd generic on SOFR and the 3rd on SONIA — the front is not even where most risk sits on a typical day. It takes four contracts to reach 45% and eight to reach 75–79%. So a front-vs-next roll relabels 11% of the book four times a year and calls the remaining 89% unchanged; the quantity it measures is not positioning in the curve.

Surviving from Part 1: the OI-migration idea (generalised), the same-day gap rule for any splice, and RAS itself unchanged. What changes is the object made continuous. A strip is a term structure, not an asset, so I would build constant-maturity continuations: for each target tenor (3m, 6m, 1y, 2y out) interpolate between the two bracketing quarterlies by time-to-expiry. That removes the roll entirely — weights move continuously, so there is no discontinuity to adjust. Positioning becomes (i) total strip OI, already roll-invariant; (ii) the OI-weighted centre of mass of the strip in months-to-expiry, the natural "where on the curve is the risk" statistic and the thing that actually moves when the hiking path reprices; (iii) pack aggregates (whites/reds/greens), because that is the traded unit. All of it DV01-weighted, not lot-weighted: a 4th-year lot and a front lot are not the same exposure, and lot counts assume they are. Caveat: deep-strip OI is 14.6% missing on SOFR beyond the 9th generic, so any strip aggregate needs an explicit coverage check first.

8. Where this breaks, and how I would catch it in production

  • Stale data defeats the rule silently. The provider forward-fills a generic's OI when the underlying has not started reporting: at the switch, generic 2 keeps the previous generic-2 contract's OI (TY, 2020-06-22: 3,302,975 lots for seven sessions); and on Gilt, OAT, Schatz and Bobl generic-2 OI freezes for up to 47 sessions at a level comparable to generic 1. Unfiltered, the front share sits at a meaningless ~0.5 and the detector fires a roll immediately after the previous one. I drop a value when the ticker changed but the value did not, or when it is the 3rd member of a run of identical sessions; front-month OI on TY/RX/US/OE never trips this (max run 2), so the filter costs nothing where the data is good. Monitor: alert when reported OI(2) exceeds curve aggregate (it did on Gilt), on any identical-OI run > 2 sessions, and on front-share flatlines.
  • The thresholds are calibrated to a two-contract world. Run unchanged on WTI they fire zero rolls in 82 cycles, because crude's front share never exceeds 0.76 — OI is spread down a monthly curve (front = 14% of aggregate). This is the right failure: the rule declines rather than guessing. Monitor: alert on any cycle reaching its provider switch with no detected roll date, and on max(s) per cycle < 0.85.
  • The same-day gap is a one-observation estimate. It inherits any settlement-timing mismatch between legs and, on a CTD switch, a jump that is economics rather than noise. Monitor: z-score each roll gap against that contract's trailing distribution; flag anything beyond 3σ (this is what would have caught the IK behaviour in section 6). A 3–5 day VWAP-weighted gap is the obvious hardening.
  • It assumes two liquid contracts and a stable cycle. A spec change, a new contract, a dislocation that halts the roll, or a wrong holiday calendar all break it. Monitor: run the calendar rule in parallel daily and alert on divergence > 2 sessions — two independent definitions disagreeing is the cheapest reliable canary. RAS itself is a ratio of medians over ~27 events and moves with the volatility regime, so I track it as a rolling 8-roll statistic with its permutation band, not a single number.

9. With unlimited time

Rebuild from individual contract histories rather than generics, removing the provider's roll timing from the problem entirely. Roll over 3–5 sessions with volume weights instead of one date, which is what an executable position does and which cuts the sensitivity to any single settlement. Make the positioning series DV01-weighted, and decompose OI into liquidity-provision and informed components following Bessembinder & Seguin, so that "conviction" is not just "more contracts". Validate externally against CFTC Commitments of Traders: if my roll-invariant OI measures positioning, it should track the COT aggregate through the roll — a testable claim I have not yet made. Finally test the end point that matters: does a signal on the adjusted series beat the same signal on the raw generic out of sample, after costs? Everything above is a prerequisite, not a result.
